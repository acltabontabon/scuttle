02 It shows its working

Every find comes with its reasons.
Every move, with a look first.

Scuttle keeps three questions apart: how sure it is about what something is, what moving it could disrupt, and whether that's yours to decide. Your own files always are.

Installed apps are left alone.

Scuttle recognises an application by how it is built — an updater beside its versions, a program beside its libraries, an app bundle — whatever a registry says and whether or not it's running. It is never suggested, and never swept up with anything else.

03 It goes in the drawer

Nothing is deleted. It's put away.

Picked things move to a holding folder, and Scuttle writes down where each came from. Putting one back is one button — into the folder it left, recreated if it's gone, beside anything newer rather than over it.

Caches and re-downloadable installers let themselves go after the days you choose. Everything else stays until you say.

04 It lives in the corner

A burrow in your menu bar.

If you want it there, Scuttle keeps a small icon in the menu bar or system tray. Click it and Scuttle peeks out with where things stand. It never starts anything by being opened, never shouts, and goes quiet the moment you ask it to.

? Reasonable questions

You probably want to know…

Does it delete anything by itself?

No. Picking something moves it to the drawer, after a review of exactly what will move. Emptying the drawer is a separate action you confirm. Keys, password vaults, browser profiles, mail, repositories and cloud-sync folders are refused outright — never even walked.

What does it look for?

Leftovers from software you removed, screenshots nobody looked at again, installers that already did their job, byte-identical copies, caches it can trace to their owner, unusually large files, and build debris if you switch it on. What it can't name goes into a pile called Oddments rather than being dressed up as a conclusion.

Could it break an app?

It's built not to. Anything shaped like an installed or portable application is recognised and left out of suggestions and batches. You can still move an application folder yourself, one at a time, with a plain warning that it will probably stop working until you put it back.

Does the drawer free up space?

Not yet. The drawer is a folder on the same disk, so a held file takes up what it did before. The space comes back when you empty the drawer — and not a moment earlier.

Does it phone home?

No account, no server, no telemetry. The one request it makes is asking GitHub, about once a day, whether a newer version exists — and it always asks you before downloading or restarting. A switch in Settings turns even that off.

Why does my Mac or PC warn me about it?

There's no paid code-signing certificate behind it, so neither system can say who made it. Once each:

macOS — open it from Applications, then System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway.

Windows — SmartScreen: More info, then Run anyway. No administrator prompt.

Nothing here asks you to switch a protection off. The installation notes go further.
