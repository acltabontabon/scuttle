A little creature
for the files
you forgot about.

Your computer keeps things. Installers from last spring, eleven attempts at the same screenshot, folders belonging to software you removed months ago. Scuttle rummages through the corners where all of that collects, and shows you what turned up — along with why it noticed.

It never deletes anything on its own. Most of what it finds is yours to keep; the point is to see it.

Four things happen, in this order

1One

It rummages

Press the button and Scuttle walks the folders you have enabled — Downloads, the Desktop, application support and cache directories, screenshot folders, Steam and Epic libraries where they exist. What it finds settles into piles you can dig through.

It narrates as it goes, and a rummage of a tidy machine comes back saying so.

2Two

It shows its working

Every finding carries the evidence behind it, and a confidence separate from a risk. Scuttle can be completely certain about what something is and still have no business suggesting you remove it.

When it cannot work out what something is, it says “Scuttle has no idea what this is. You decide.” rather than guessing.

3Three

It goes in the drawer

Nothing you pick is deleted. It moves to a holding folder and Scuttle writes down where it came from, so putting it back is one button — into exactly the folder it left, recreated if you have since deleted it, and never on top of something that has taken its name.

The drawer is still on your disk, so it has not freed any space yet. Scuttle says so on the drawer screen, in those words.

These files still take up disk space. Put them back before they expire.

The drawer

4Four

Or you let it go

Emptying the drawer is the only thing that frees the space, and the only thing that destroys anything. Items do not go to the Trash or the Recycle Bin — the confirmation says exactly that before you agree to it.

Anything you leave in the drawer expires after the window you choose — 7, 14 or 30 days — and is removed the next time Scuttle starts.

Reasonable questions

What does it look for?

Leftovers from software that is no longer installed, screenshots nobody looked at again, installers that already did their job, byte-identical duplicates, caches it can attribute to the application that made them, unusually large files, and developer build debris if you switch it on. Anything it notices but cannot name goes into a pile called Oddments rather than being dressed up as a conclusion.

Does it delete anything by itself?

No. Picking something moves it to the drawer, and emptying the drawer is a separate, confirmed action you take. Scuttle also refuses to touch whole categories of place outright — keys, password vaults, browser profiles, mail, repositories, cloud-sync folders — and those are not merely hidden from the results, they are never walked at all.

Does putting something in the drawer free up space?

Not yet. The drawer is a folder on the same disk, so a held file still occupies exactly what it did before. The space comes back when you empty the drawer, and not a moment earlier.

Can I get something back?

Yes — that is what the drawer is for. Put it back returns a file to where it came from. If the folder it lived in has since gone, Scuttle recreates it; if something else has taken its name, Scuttle restores it alongside rather than over the top, and tells you where it went.

Does it work offline?

It only works offline, in effect. There is no account, no server and no telemetry, and Scuttle makes no network requests of its own — it does not even check whether it is out of date. Findings and drawer records live in one database on your machine, and full paths are never written to its logs.

Why does my Mac or PC warn me about it?

Because Scuttle is built without paid code-signing certificates, so neither operating system can tell you who made it. The macOS builds are not Developer ID signed or notarized, and the Windows installer is unsigned.

On macOS, open it once from Applications, then go to System Settings → Privacy & Security, scroll to Security, and click Open Anyway. That button appears only after you have tried to open the app, and only for about an hour afterwards.

On Windows, SmartScreen will say it stopped an unrecognised app: click More info, then Run anyway. It installs for your account only, so there is no administrator prompt.

You will not be asked to switch anything off. The installation notes go through both in more detail, including how to tell an ordinary warning from a genuinely broken download.

Go on, then. See what it drags out.
