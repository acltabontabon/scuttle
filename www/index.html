Four things happen, in this order

1One

It rummages

Press the button. Scuttle walks the folders you have enabled — Downloads, Desktop, application support, caches, screenshots, Steam and Epic — and what it finds settles into piles.

A rummage of a tidy machine comes back saying so.

2Two

It shows its working

Every finding carries its evidence, and a confidence separate from a risk. Scuttle can be certain what something is and still have no business suggesting you remove it.

When it cannot tell, it says “Scuttle has no idea what this is. You decide.”

3Three

It goes in the drawer

Nothing you pick is deleted. It moves to a holding folder, and Scuttle writes down where it came from — so putting it back is one button, into the folder it left, recreated if you have since deleted it.

The drawer is still on your disk. No space has come back yet, and Scuttle says so.

The drawer

4Four

Or you let it go

Emptying the drawer is the only thing that frees the space, and the only thing that destroys anything. Nothing goes to the Trash, and the confirmation says so before you agree to it.

Whatever you leave expires after 7, 14 or 30 days — your choice. It goes at the next start, or soon after the date if you keep Scuttle in the menu bar.

Delete all 3 things permanently? This frees 1.2 GB. Nothing goes to the Trash and nothing can be recovered.

Reasonable questions

What does it look for?

Leftovers from software you removed, screenshots nobody looked at again, installers that already did their job, byte-identical copies, caches it can trace to the app that made them, unusually large files, and build debris if you switch it on. What it cannot name goes into a pile called Oddments rather than being dressed up as a conclusion.

Does it delete anything by itself?

No. Picking something moves it to the drawer; emptying the drawer is a separate action you confirm. Whole categories of place are refused outright — keys, password vaults, browser profiles, mail, repositories, cloud-sync folders — and not merely hidden from the results. They are never walked at all.

Does putting something in the drawer free up space?

Not yet. The drawer is a folder on the same disk, so a held file takes up exactly what it did before. The space comes back when you empty the drawer, and not a moment earlier.

Can I get something back?

Yes — that is what the drawer is for. Put it back returns a file to where it came from. If that folder has since gone, Scuttle recreates it; if something has taken its name, Scuttle restores alongside rather than over the top, and says where.

Does it sit in my menu bar?

Only if you ask. Left alone Scuttle quits when you close the window and does nothing at all in between. Turning it on keeps it behind a menu bar icon — a system tray icon on Windows — and ⌘Q, logging out and shutting down still quit it properly.

Will it scan on its own?

Not unless you switch that on separately, and then at most once a day, when the machine looks able to spare it. A background check reads names, sizes and dates and never opens a file, so it cannot find duplicates or near-identical screenshots — Scuttle says so on the screen rather than letting an empty pile read as good news. It moves nothing and removes nothing.

It is not watching you. Scuttle asks for no accessibility, input monitoring or screen recording permission, and a quiet machine on mains power is treated as a reasonable moment to try, not as proof that you have gone.

Does it work offline?

Only, in effect. No account, no server, no telemetry, and no network requests of its own — it does not even check whether it is out of date. Findings live in one database on your machine, and full paths never reach its logs.

Why does my Mac or PC warn me about it?

There are no paid code-signing certificates behind it, so neither system can tell you who made it. Once each:

macOS — open it from Applications, then System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway. That button only appears after you have tried to open the app, and only for about an hour.

Windows — SmartScreen says it stopped an unrecognised app: More info, then Run anyway. No administrator prompt.

Nothing here asks you to switch a protection off. The installation notes go further, including how to tell an ordinary warning from a genuinely broken download.

Go on, then. See what it drags out.
